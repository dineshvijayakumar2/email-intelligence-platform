## What this is

For each customer, this gives one clear recommendation - **what product to pitch, which contact to approach, the context to use, and when to reach out** - along with how confident we are and the evidence behind it.

**How the recommendation is made.** We look at what each customer currently buys, then find customers with a similar buying pattern and see what *they* buy that this customer doesn't. If customers who buy the same things reliably also buy a product this customer has never ordered, that product is the recommendation. The "who, when, and context" then come from this customer's own order history and email activity.

**The two numbers on every recommendation:**

- **Confidence** answers: *of the customers similar to this one, what share also buy the recommended product?* A confidence of 70% means 7 in 10 similar customers buy it - a strong, reliable signal. 30% means only about 1 in 3 do - a real but weaker bet.
- **Lift** answers: *are these customers genuinely more likely to buy it, or is it just a popular product anyway?* A lift of 2.0 means customers like this one are twice as likely as the average customer to buy it - that's a targeted insight. A lift near 1.0 means the product is just broadly popular, so the recommendation isn't really telling you anything specific. Those low-lift ones are flagged, not featured.

The best recommendations score high on **both**: a strong share of similar customers buy it (confidence), *and* that's well above the normal rate (lift).

**Timing** is judged against each customer's *own* ordering pace - how many days they typically leave between orders. "Due soon" or "overdue" means they're past their usual gap and likely ready to reorder; "in cadence" means they're still within their normal rhythm.

**Honest notes:**

- Confidence is the real pattern percentage, never rounded up or relabelled.
- Before a product is recommended, we confirm the customer has genuinely never ordered it - checked across the whole company's order history, not just one contact or site.
- Recommendations are based on purchase patterns only - no account-manager scorecards or assumptions.
- Companies with unresolved data-quality flags are sunk in the ranking, so none reached this list.
- Some accounts are managed through mailboxes pending re-authentication; their timing/recency may be understated (a stale-looking account can be a sync gap, not silence).
- Pitches concentrate on Embellishment and Wide Format because those are the strongest cross-sell signals in the book - a real structural pattern, covered in the closing section.

## The pattern behind the recommendations

Two structural findings shape where cross-sell effort should go:

**Embellishment and Wide Format are the genuine next purchase for customers who buy books and display work.** This is the real association structure of the book, not an artefact of the method - customers who buy Soft Cover / Hard Cover Books and Display/Installation reliably go on to add Embellishment and Wide Format. It is the single most actionable cross-sell direction in the data, which is why those two pitches dominate the strong tier.

**A number of high-revenue accounts already buy everything we'd recommend to them** - there's no gap left to fill - **and several more already apply embellishment finishes within their existing jobs.** These are retention accounts, not cross-sell targets; pitching them a product they effectively already use would waste the outreach. Identifying them is as valuable as identifying the openings, because it tells the team where not to spend effort. (During validation, 17 of 30 initial Embellishment pitches were found to already apply finishes within jobs and were corrected out - the reason the recommendations you see are genuine gaps.)

## What's next - turning recommendations into outreach, and coaching the approach

This set is the first half of the picture: *which* opportunities exist. Two pieces follow.

**Draft-ready outreach.** Each recommendation here is already structured to become an actual email - the product, the contact, the context, and the timing are all captured. The next step is generating a drafted message for each, placed in the account manager's drafts folder for them to review, adjust, and send. Nothing sends automatically; the AM stays in control. This turns a list of opportunities into something an account manager can act on in minutes rather than research themselves.

**Account-manager approach analysis.** The second piece looks at *how* outreach and customer conversations actually convert - what distinguishes the approaches that win work from those that don't, drawn from the email history itself. The aim is practical: surface what's working so it can be applied more widely, and give each account manager useful, specific signal rather than a scorecard. This is a different kind of analysis from the cross-sell engine (it works at the level of conversations and threads), and it's the most direct lever on lifting outreach performance across the team. It depends on completing the email-history processing already underway, so it follows this set rather than running alongside it.

Together, the three pieces form one system: *what to offer* (this set), *how to say it* (draft-ready outreach), and *what approach wins* (the conversation analysis).

## Method

Capability is defined as qb-OR-classifier (qb_capability_tag else classifier capability_tags), >=2 jobs for rule basket. For each customer we pick the single best product they've never ordered, ranked by confidence × lift, and only keep it if the pattern is backed by at least 30 similar customers, at least 25% confidence, and a lift above 1 (genuinely more likely than average). Standalone-Embellishment pitches are suppressed when the company already applies two or more embellishment finishes within jobs. Timing is judged against each customer's own ordering pace - comparing how long it's been since their last order to how long they typically leave between orders - plus any seasonal pattern and recent email activity. Ranking dampens revenue (log scale) so confidence, lift, and timing move the order, which surfaces small-but-potent accounts alongside large ones.

*All figures come from QuickBase order history and email engagement data. Before listing any recommendation, we confirmed the customer has never ordered the product anywhere across their account; the top 15 were also checked by hand against the raw QuickBase records.*
